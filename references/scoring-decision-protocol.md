# Scoring decision protocol

Use this protocol to reduce praise bias, category leakage, and score drift. It does not make the result objective; it makes the reasoning inspectable and repeatable.

## Pass 1: evidence inventory

Before assigning numbers, record only visible facts:

- probable subject or visual center
- decisive gesture, expression, alignment, light change, or spatial event
- foreground, middle-ground, and background relations
- brightest, highest-contrast, and most saturated competing areas
- edge collisions, mergers, blocked gestures, and accidental tangencies
- repeated shapes, sight lines, color echoes, and directional movement
- focus, motion, exposure, crop, and processing consequences

Separate each non-visible claim as `confirmed` or `inferred`. Do not use an inferred claim to raise a score.

## Pass 2: attempt statement

Complete one sentence internally:

> This photograph appears to depend on ___, made visible by ___.

If the first blank is clear but the second cannot be supported, reduce confidence. If both are unclear, do not compensate with atmospheric language.

## Pass 3: independent category scores

Score the six categories without copying one success across the sheet.

- A rare gesture may raise moment but does not prove strong composition.
- Clean geometry may raise composition but does not prove human meaning.
- Multiple planes may raise layering only when they interact.
- Attractive light may raise light/color but does not prove originality.
- An unusual subject may remain generic observation.
- Sharpness and exposure cannot rescue a missing event.

For every category above 80% of its maximum, identify one category-specific piece of visible evidence. If none exists, lower that category.

## Pass 4: counter-tests

### Replaceability test

Ask whether a nearby exposure or another competent photographer could easily produce an equivalent frame. Easy replaceability caps originality and usually limits the total to the competent range.

### One-second-later test

Ask what would change one second earlier or later. If almost nothing changes, do not describe the timing as decisive. Quiet photographs may pass through a light, spacing, or gaze change rather than peak action.

### Subject-removal test

Mentally remove the most unusual, emotional, famous, vulnerable, or visually striking subject. If little photographic organization remains, the image depends on subject matter more than authorship.

### Monochrome/color test

Ask what color contributes. If color does not organize attention, temperature, depth, contradiction, or information, do not award high color points merely for attractiveness.

### Squint and edge test

Squint to check the main visual hierarchy, then inspect all four edges. A strong center does not cancel a damaging border, competing highlight, or accidental merger.

### Repeated-viewing test

After the first impression, identify what remains to discover. Surprise without durable structure should not enter the exceptional bands.

## Pass 5: total-score gates

These are evidence gates, not automatic deductions.

### Before awarding 80 or above

Require all of the following:

- a clear event, relation, observation, or atmospheric transformation
- intentional visual organization with no major unresolved distraction
- at least one quality beyond technical cleanliness or attractive subject matter
- a specific weakness that explains why the frame is not in a higher band

### Before awarding 85 or above

Also require:

- strong evidence in at least four of the five non-technical categories
- visible authorial judgment that is difficult to replace with a nearby exposure
- no major contradiction between the numerical score and the written criticism
- enough structure, tension, or ambiguity to survive repeated viewing

### Before awarding 90 or above

Also require:

- timing, form, meaning, light, or context reinforcing one another rather than succeeding separately
- no major weakness and only minor, non-central limitations
- rare staying power after the initial subject or surprise is understood
- a written reason explaining what makes the photograph exceptional rather than merely excellent

Reserve 95–100 for historical-caliber territory. Do not use it for the strongest frame in an otherwise weak batch.

## Pass 6: score-language consistency

- **Below 60:** the critique must identify a foundational failure, not only small refinements.
- **60–69:** acknowledge the intention but explain why it remains unresolved.
- **70–79:** identify the photograph's real competence and the missing factor preventing serious selection.
- **80–84:** describe a clear success and the visible limitation holding it below outstanding.
- **85–89:** explain the distinctive construction and the small remaining limitation.
- **90–94:** explain rarity, integration, and staying power; generic superlatives are insufficient.

If the prose sounds much stronger or weaker than the number, revise one of them.

## Batch normalization

1. Score every photograph absolutely before ranking.
2. Re-open the provisional strongest, median, and weakest frames together.
3. Check whether identical flaws received comparable consequences.
4. For photographs within two points, treat the numerical order as provisional and use confidence, the decisive visible difference, and the requested editing goal.
5. Never force a batch into a bell curve and never award a high score merely because one frame is best in the set.

## Near-duplicate decision

Compare in this order:

1. decisive gesture or gaze
2. clarity of the central relationship
3. subject separation and mergers
4. background and edge distractions
5. spacing, rhythm, and repeated forms
6. light, reflection, and color alignment
7. focus or micro-technical difference

Choose the frame with the strongest meaningful relationship, not automatically the sharpest one. If neither frame resolves the event, say so rather than declaring a false winner.

## Confidence

- **High:** the attempt and evidence are clear; image quality is sufficient; the main conclusion survives the counter-tests.
- **Medium:** the frame supports more than one plausible reading, a key detail is small, or close alternatives remain.
- **Low:** resolution, crop, context, or visual ambiguity prevents a dependable judgment.

Confidence measures certainty in the evaluation, not photographic quality.

## Forward-test procedure for future revisions

When enough user-approved photographs and scores exist, maintain a private calibration set rather than adding copyrighted reference images to the repository.

- Include strong, borderline, and rejected frames.
- Include near-duplicate pairs and quiet images, not only obvious peak moments.
- Record the creator's keep/reject decision and decisive reason separately from the model's score.
- Re-run the set after major rubric changes without showing previous scores to the evaluator.
- Investigate repeated disagreements; do not change the rubric for a single outlier.
- Keep test photographs out of public releases unless the rights holder explicitly authorizes publication.
