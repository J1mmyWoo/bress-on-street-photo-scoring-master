# Historical scoring calibration

Checked against official museum and Magnum sources on 2026-07-16.

Use historically important photographs to calibrate qualities and scoring severity, never as templates to copy. A new photograph receives no points for superficial resemblance to a famous image.

## What enduring photographs demonstrate

### Timing and formal echo

Henri Cartier-Bresson's `Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare` joins an unrepeatable instant with formal repetition: the suspended heel, still water, reflection, circular ripples, and a leaping figure on a background poster. Moment and composition reinforce each other.

Source: [MoMA audio guide](https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/244/3135)

Scoring lesson: award very high moment scores only when timing changes the meaning or structure of the frame. A person merely walking through a clean composition is not automatically a decisive moment.

### Compression and emotional force

Dorothea Lange's `Migrant Mother` removes nonessential information and uses gesture, faces turned away, and physical closeness to concentrate attention. Its power also depends on real historical circumstances. Later disagreement about parts of Lange's account warns that a famous image does not excuse uncertain caption facts.

Source: [MoMA collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/50989)

Scoring lesson: emotional force must come from the visible frame and verified context, not an invented backstory. Strong compression removes distraction without flattening a person into a symbol.

### Personal viewpoint and social observation

Robert Frank's `Trolley, New Orleans` organizes separate passengers within streetcar windows while carrying a distinct view of American social life. Its authorship lies in selection, framing, sequencing, and point of view rather than perfect technical polish.

Source: [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/285923)

Scoring lesson: originality means the photograph contains the photographer's judgment. Familiar subject matter can score highly when relationships, framing, and social observation feel specific.

### Editing and the near-miss

Magnum's examination of Richard Kalvar's `Woman in the Window` shows how small changes in angle, background clutter, reflection, and gesture separate the chosen frame from weaker neighboring exposures.

Source: [Magnum Photos contact-sheet analysis](https://www.magnumphotos.com/theory-and-practice/contact-sheet-richard-kalvar-woman-window/)

Scoring lesson: compare near-duplicates closely. Choose the frame with the clearest relationship and fewest accidental distractions. A series of almost-good exposures does not make any single frame strong.

### Photography as interpretation

MoMA notes that composition, inclusion, exclusion, cropping, editing, and alteration all introduce a point of view.

Source: [MoMA, Photography as Witness](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/photography/photography-as-witness)

Scoring lesson: technical neutrality does not equal truth. Reward visible authorship while checking that processing and descriptive claims remain honest.

## Absolute anchors

- **95–100:** Reserve for an image in which moment, form, human meaning, authorship, and staying power are all extraordinary. Do not use this range as encouragement.
- **90–94:** Rare. The frame survives repeated viewing, has no major visual weakness, and feels difficult to replace with a nearby exposure.
- **85–89:** Outstanding. Strong personal voice and precise construction, with only small limitations.
- **80–84:** Clearly successful and worth serious consideration, though a visible weakness keeps it below exceptional territory.
- **70–79:** Coherent or competent work with a meaningful weakness in moment, originality, emotional force, layering, or spatial order.
- **60–69:** The intention can be recognized, but the observation or arrangement remains familiar and unresolved.
- **Below 60:** Weak timing, unresolved composition, generic observation, misleading processing, or insufficient visual interest.

## Guardrails

- Score the image, not the photographer's intention, equipment, reputation, travel distance, or access to an unusual subject.
- Do not award hypothetical historical significance to a new photograph.
- Do not penalize grain, blur, tilt, deep shadow, flare, or imperfect focus when they serve the image.
- Penalize those same qualities when they obscure the central relationship without adding meaning.
- Treat humour, beauty, sadness, and shock as neutral. Their presence does not guarantee quality.
- Require more than graphic neatness: a well-composed photograph can still be emotionally empty.
- Require more than emotion: a touching subject can still be photographed generically.
